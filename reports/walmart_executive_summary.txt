Between February 2010 and October 2012, Walmart's 45-store network generated a
combined $6.74 billion in weekly sales across 81 departments. At first glance,
that's a story of scale and consistency. The average weekly sales figure of
$15,981 per department record reflects the steady throughput you'd expect from a
retail operation this size. But beneath that aggregate number is a more nuanced
picture: a handful of stores carry disproportionate weight, seasonal forces drive
dramatic swings, and a notable share of locations show signs of sustained revenue
decline that leadership should be paying attention to.

The top performers are concentrated at the high end of the portfolio. Store 20
leads the network at $301.4 million in total sales over the analysis period,
followed by Store 4 at $299.5 million, Store 14 at $289.0 million, Store 13 at
$286.5 million, and Store 2 at $275.4 million. Together, these five locations
account for a striking share of network revenue, which reinforces that Walmart's
strongest stores function as anchor assets. Their scale, store type, and
operational consistency set them apart from the long tail of smaller, more
volatile performers, and they represent the clearest benchmarks for what
high-functioning retail execution looks like across the chain.

Seasonality plays a defining role in shaping Walmart's revenue rhythm. December
consistently came in as the highest-performing month, with average weekly sales
reaching $19,355.70, nearly $5,000 above January's network low of $14,126.08.
That gap shows just how sharply consumer behavior shifts between holiday peak and
post-holiday pullback. Holiday weeks across the full dataset averaged $17,035.82
compared to $15,901.45 for non-holiday weeks, a 7.1% lift that confirms how
much key calendar events matter commercially. At this scale, that margin is not
trivial. It represents hundreds of millions of dollars in incremental performance
driven purely by timing, and it means inventory, staffing, and promotional
strategies need to be calibrated carefully around those windows.

Volatility analysis adds another layer to the risk picture. Five stores stand
out as the most erratic performers in the network: Store 35, Store 7, Store 15,
Store 29, and Store 23, with coefficients of variation of 22.9%, 19.7%, 19.3%,
18.4%, and 17.9% respectively. A high coefficient of variation means week-to-week
sales fluctuate wildly relative to each store's own average, making these
locations hard to plan for and operationally expensive to manage. Store 35 in
particular, with a CV nearly double that of the most stable locations, is a real
forecasting challenge. That kind of variability could be masking underlying
structural issues, whether driven by local market conditions, inconsistent
execution, or external economic pressures in its trade area.

The most operationally significant finding may be how widespread high-risk
decline patterns are across the network. Using weekly sales decline frequency as
a proxy for structural weakness, 13 of the 45 stores were flagged as high-risk
for exceeding the 76th percentile threshold for consecutive or recurring revenue
contraction. That group includes Store 28 (86 declining weeks), Store 42 (85
declining weeks), and both Store 38 and Store 20 (82 declining weeks each). The
fact that Store 20, a top-five revenue generator, shows up in this cohort is
worth noting. Raw sales volume alone is not a reliable indicator of operational
health. These 13 locations should be treated as priority candidates for targeted
operational review, examining whether store-specific factors like labor
efficiency, local competition, markdown strategy, or demographic shifts are
driving persistent downward pressure that the aggregate metrics are obscuring.

The data points to a network performing at scale but with meaningful unevenness
underneath. The top five stores provide a reliable revenue foundation, and the
holiday calendar continues to deliver dependable lift. But the volatility
concentrated in nearly a dozen stores, combined with decline patterns affecting
more than a quarter of the network, suggests the next phase of strategic
investment should focus less on the stores already winning. The real opportunity
is in understanding what's holding the rest of the portfolio back and closing
the gap between Walmart's best locations and those still operating well below
their potential.
